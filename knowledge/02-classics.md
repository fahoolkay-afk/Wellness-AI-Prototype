## 1. Classical lesionology

### 1.1 What counts as a lesion
- **Tasker:** "Any structural change which affects the functional activity of any tissue is called a lesion." A structural variant that leaves function intact is *not* a lesion. [TAS ch. II]
- **The three signs of a true lesion:** (1) it can be felt, (2) the functional disturbance links to it anatomically and physiologically, (3) there is **hyperaesthesia at the palpable area**. In practice they are noted in the reverse of their historical order: **structure, then tension, then tenderness**. [TAS ch. II]
- **Tenderness decides it.** Small deviations and muscle tension are present "in practically all people, sick or well." An active lesion is a deviation **plus** tension **plus** tenderness in the same segments. [TAS ch. II]
- **Loss of motion matters more than position.** Spinal joints glide, so "it isn't the position which constitutes the lesion so much as it is the loss of motion." Lesions are partial fixations. [TAS ch. II]
- **Littlejohn:** "There is no purely mechanical lesion… The lesion is not in the position, or rather malposition, but in the mal-relation of these structures, or the mal-activity of the organism." His definition: "any change from the normal in structural relations, activity correlations, or environing conditions that reacts upon the organism… sufficiently to produce vital unhealth." [LES pp. 1–29]
- **Littlejohn's lesion tissues:** (a) contracture, hardening, thickening or undue softening of soft tissue (muscle, fascia, cartilage); (b) small changes in hard-tissue relations (bone, ligament, articular); (c) changes in the environment. Lesions can be mechanical, chemical or toxic. [LES pp. 1–43]
- **Modern restatement:** Somatic dysfunction shows as altered range and quality of movement, altered tissue texture, and sometimes asymmetry. "In the absence of other features… vertebral misalignment is of no significance." [OD ch. 2]

### 1.2 Lesion types
| Axis | Types | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Change in **position** (subluxation, fixation) vs change in **size** (thickened ligament, callus, fibrosis). Size changes are harder to undo because the underlying change runs deeper. | [TAS ch. II] |
| Cause | **Violence**: sudden, or a weak force kept up over time (fatigue, posture) → *primary*. **Failure of adaptation**: to temperature, clothing, labour, food, emotion → *secondary*. **Chemical/dietary**: food that is hard to digest or eaten while fatigued gives spinal lesions that are "objective symptoms of visceral disease". | [TAS ch. II] |
| Origin | **Primary** (traumatic, causal) vs **secondary** (the body's adaptive and compensatory sign, and itself a cause of new disorder elsewhere). | [TAS ch. III; HUL; BUR] |
| Stage | **Acute**: warm, boggy, moist, rigid board-like tension, worst tenderness, oedema, red reflex that persists. **Chronic**: slightly warm or cool, thin and smooth, dry, ropy/stringy, less tender, no oedema, redness fades fast or blanches. | [OD Table 2.1] |
| Littlejohn general-case "key lesions" | C1, C4, D2, D5, D10, L2, L5 ± sacrum in chronic, multi-cause patients. | [LES Kemp p. 70] |
| Accommodated | An old lesion the body has adapted to (e.g. new femoral socket after dislocation). "If the body has succeeded in recuperating… it is unwise to disturb them." | [TAS ch. II; TAS13 ch. XVI] |

### 1.3 Telling primary from compensatory or secondary lesions (pre-session checklist)
Tasker: a lesion "can be classed as primary or secondary only after careful study of all those factors which constitute the history and symptomatology." [TAS ch. III]

**Points toward PRIMARY (causal, treat directly):**
1. **History of an accident that bears directly on the lesion.** Ask first: "Is there any history of accident?" Tasker's street-car wrench at T12/L1 was followed by constipation and T12–L1 dermatomal symptoms. [TAS ch. II]
2. **The lesion recurs at the same site with its old features** (tenderness, rigidity, loss of motion) after weather changes or exertion. [TAS ch. II]
3. **It explains distant symptoms through its segment.** Chronic illness whose "original lesion… [was] lost to view" through compensations is the classic osteopathic finding. [TAS ch. II]
4. **It is the dominant, most fixed restriction**, with rigidity in the segments above and below protecting it. [TAS ch. II]
5. **Chronic texture** (ropy, dry, thin; see table) at a key junction (C0–1, C7–T1, T12–L1, L5–S1). [OD; TAS]

**Points toward SECONDARY / COMPENSATORY (look elsewhere first):**
1. **No history of trauma.** Tasker warns against forcing an "obscure trauma" explanation onto every lesion. [TAS ch. III]
2. **Visceral symptoms come first or run alongside.** The lesion sits at the segment that innervates an organ with symptoms (see §2). Spinal lesions "multiply and intensify in proportion to the extent and severity" of the disease, and spread to neighbouring segments as other organs take on compensatory work (e.g. stomach → liver/pancreas segments). [TAS ch. III]
3. **It returns after correction** "so long as environmental forces are overwhelming." Tasker: secondary lesions "may be removed by manipulation… but will return again." Littlejohn: "if the bone lesion were the only lesion… after correction it would remain corrected." [TAS ch. II; LES]
4. **It is part of a curve.** In a lateral curvature "the physician must determine which curve is primary and which is compensatory." Treating a compensatory curve without addressing the short leg or hip is "useless." [TAS13 ch. XVI] Hall: about 75% of lumbo-dorsal convexities are functional compensations for an L5 or pelvic lesion and respond to treatment. [LES p. 64]
5. **It counter-tenses a primary.** A T12/L1 lesion → psoas, diaphragm and abdominal counter-tension → reduced breathing and bowel action. [TAS ch. II]
6. **Pelvic organ "malpositions"** (prolapsed ovary, retroverted uterus, tense uterosacral ligaments) are "in the majority of cases, secondary reactions to the spinal lesion field." [LES Littlejohn]

**Visceral vs musculoskeletal red flags** [OD ch. 2–3]: pain behaviour that does not fit mechanics, plus organ signs (dysuria/frequency → kidney; diarrhoea → bowel; altered menses → gynaecological). **Protective postures:** a lateral shift from kidney infection (side-bending toward the centre is *not* necessarily painful, unlike a disc); flexion deformity with an acute abdomen. In cancer, osteopathy is "inappropriate and, if forceful techniques were used, potentially harmful." If a disc injury is possible, use gentle technique.

**Hulett/Burns rule:** remove secondary lesions as well, because they "constitute new causal factors", but alongside hygiene and environmental correction. [HUL; BUR]

### 1.4 Jarvis decision rule (synthesised from the above)
`If trauma history + local fixed restriction + chronic texture → treat as primary, specifically.`
`If visceral/organ complaint + tender segment matching §2 + no trauma → treat as secondary: lighten the segment (inhibition/soft tissue), address the organ's drivers, and refer if red flags are present.`
`If curve/asymmetry → find the primary curve or leg-length/pelvic driver before treating the compensating curve.`
`If old, adapted and asymptomatic → leave it alone.`

## 2. Segment ↔ organ map (viscerosomatic / somatovisceral)

### 2.1 Mechanisms (Pottenger) [POT ch. V–VI]
- **Visceromotor reflex:** afferents from an inflamed organ travel via sympathetic sensory fibres to the cord and produce **spasm of skeletal muscle** in the same segment. Often only *part* of a muscle is involved (e.g. a band of rectus in gastric ulcer). It can last months (shoulder girdle in pulmonary TB, lumbar muscles in renal TB).
- **Viscerosensory reflex:** **pain or hyperalgesia in skin, subcutis and muscle** of the segment. Cord cells become hyperirritable, so ordinary stimuli register as pain.
- **Viscerotrophic reflex:** **degeneration** (atrophy, thinning) of skin, subcutis and muscle when visceral irritation continues. A visceromotor spasm left in place "passes over into degeneration." → In an intake, *wasting or tissue thinning at a segment means a long-standing visceral driver.*
- **Parasympathetic reflexes** (vagal and sacral) show up as "functional disturbances" (secretion, motility).
- Body-wall segments sit **lower** than their cord segments: the T10 skin segment lies at the level of the L1 vertebra and T12 at L4. Kidney, ureter and uterus therefore refer to the *lumbar* region. [POT ch. IV]
- **Head's Law (Littlejohn):** pain from a low-sensitivity part (viscus) is felt in a closely connected high-sensitivity part (skin/muscle). "Head's Law… works also in reverse": somatic lesions alter organ function (somatovisceral). [LES p. 44]
- **Tasker:** spinal tenderness turns attention to *every* structure supplied from that segment. The T1–T7 range alone may involve lungs, pleura, heart, eyes or stomach, so other examination is needed to decide which. [TAS ch. XI]

## 3. Principles from Still and Tasker that translate into action

1. **Listen anatomically.** While taking the history, Still mapped symptoms onto bones and joints, then ligaments, muscles, nerves (cerebrospinal and sympathetic), arteries, veins, lymphatics and glands, "trying to determine… the structural alterations which have occurred to produce the symptoms." → Jarvis should list, for each complaint, the candidate structures in that order. [LES Jackson p. 52]
2. **"The rule of the artery is absolute, universal, and it must be unobstructed, or disease will result."** Nerves depend on arterial supply. Always ask: is anything obstructing arterial supply, venous return or lymphatic return? [AUTO 1908; LES Kemp]
3. **Fascia is where to look.** "The fascia is the place to look for the cause of disease and the place to begin the action of remedies in all diseases." [PMP; PHIL]
4. **Lymph.** "What we meet with in all diseases is dead blood, stagnant lymph…" Disease is "too much dirt in the wheels of life" and lymph washes it out. → Include lymphatic and diaphragm work when there is congestion, infection or oedema. [PMP; PHIL]
5. **Disease is an effect.** "Disease is an effect only, and a positive proof that a belt is off, a journal bent, or a cog broken." The practitioner's power goes as far as knowledge of the normal and skill "to give freedom to nerves, blood, secretions and excretions, and no farther." [R&P]
6. **Prepare the soft tissue first, then correct, then leave it alone.** Still would not correct "until the soft tissues had been prepared to the point where the correction had a reasonable chance of being maintained." He treated D. Clark's neck with soft tissue daily for three weeks before correcting. **Find it, fix it, leave it alone**: repeated force on a joint already normalised "is one of the worst services we can render." [LES Jackson pp. 56–57]
7. **Stay within physiological range.** Carry joints "through their normal physiological range of motion without force or injury"; ligaments injured past range may stay painful for life. [LES Jackson p. 52]
8. **Review the whole spine.** Still worked by section (upper cervical, lower cervical, upper/middle/lower dorsal, L1–4, L5–sacrum) but "invariably included other areas". He stressed the **sacrum–upper cervical** relationship. [LES Jackson p. 54]
9. **"Give the body back to itself" (Littlejohn).** After a specific correction, articulate the whole body. Adjust a lesion in relation to its neighbours, never "as such". Free **flexion and extension before rotation** in curves. Fix the lower vertebra and lever the upper. For scoliosis after age 14, take the case only if you can supervise it for 1–3 years. [LES Hall pp. 61–64]
10. **Three pillars (Littlejohn via Kemp).** (A) Structure: general articulation, then specific correction. (B) Function: ribs, diaphragm and lung centre D1–5; splanchnics D6–12 plus diet; elimination L2–3, SI joints, skin, fluids, lymphatic pump. (C) Environment: posture and occupation (e.g. typists' D6 strain), mental and emotional load, meaning. → A ready-made template for structuring a session plan. [LES Kemp pp. 67–72]
11. **Using inhibition and stimulation (Tasker).** Inhibition = **steady digital pressure** over a nerve trunk or over the segment linked to the target organ. It lowers sensory bombardment and so helps vascular tone return in the same segment. Tasker warns that exact dosing is impossible and that, used alone, these are palliative. Examples: phrenic inhibition for hiccough; sacral-foramina inhibition for bladder pain and perineal spasm. A short inhibition cannot undo a systemic process (Tasker's malaria chill example). [TAS13 ch. XVII; TAS ch. XI]
12. **Hilton's Law.** The nerve that supplies a joint also supplies the muscles that move it and the skin over it. So C1 links the OA joint, the suboccipitals and the scalp, and through vagal connections the larynx and pharynx: laryngeal irritation → suboccipital contraction → atlas lesion, and the reverse. [TAS ch. X–XI]
13. **Adaptation is a result, not a failure.** "It is necessary for us to decide… whether or not the present condition of the individual is as good as it can be made." Do not chase every asymmetry. Some compensations, such as diarrhoea compensating for poor kidney or skin elimination, should not be suppressed. [TAS13 ch. XVI]
14. **Is treatment appropriate? (modern four-question screen)** Is somatic dysfunction present? Is it related to the symptoms? Can it improve with treatment? Are there contraindications? OMPs "do not treat 'conditions'". [OD ch. 1]
15. **Space and position (modern reframing).** Bordoni & Escher propose "the position of a body structure decides its shape and function," and describe the aim of treatment as creating space and sliding between tissue layers so that fluid, chemistry and neural traffic can move. This is useful wording, but the quantum framing in the article is speculative. [BOR]

## 4. Cranial essentials

**From the slice [CC]:**
- **Lineage.** Still asked: "How old is osteopathy? … As old as the cranium itself." W.G. Sutherland (ASO 1898–1900) saw the bevelled sphenosquamous and temporal squama as "a design for motion… a primary respiratory mechanism." **Charlotte Weaver** (ASO 1909–12) was, by her own account, asked by Still to carry his work "into the cephalic region." She modelled skull bones as **three modified cranial vertebrae** (occiput, sphenoid, dorsum sellae / CL3–CL1). Her students' lineage runs to Arbuckle, Magoun and Upledger (CST).
- **Sutherland's five PRM components:** (1) fluctuation of CSF (the "Tide"); (2) reciprocal tension membranes; (3) motility of the neural tube; (4) articular mobility of the cranial bones; (5) involuntary motion of the sacrum between the ilia. The slides say Weaver's model makes no mention of sacrum or CSF flux.
- **Weaver's three vulnerable sites,** where the dura splits into two layers to allow motion: **sella turcica** (pituitary, so wide endocrine effects), **cavernous sinus** (venous: ophthalmic and cerebral tributaries), **foramen lacerum** (arterial: internal carotid). → Clinical reasoning prompt: relate sutural strain to the nearest foramen and what passes through it.
- **Clinical framing:** neuropsychiatric syndromes were Weaver's main clinical interest. Her "lesioned basicranium" idea extends the logic of the spinal lesion: if spinal segments can be lesioned, so can cephalic segments.
- **Still on CSF** [PMP/PHIL]: "the cerebro-spinal fluid is one of the highest known elements… and unless the brain furnishes this fluid in abundance, a disabled condition of the body will remain." **Lymphatics "drink from the waters of the brain."**
- **Upper-cervical gateway** [R&P; LES]: Still freed the vertebral artery and treated headache by gentle suboccipital and transverse-process pressure until the tissues relaxed down to C5. He adjusted atlas/axis so there is "no obstruction to the approach or the return of blood from the brain." Littlejohn's OA method: fix the atlas and rotate the occiput, without thrust where possible. "Rough handling of the neck has lost osteopathy more patients than… any other part."

**Indications and contraindications:** *The slice has no formal cranial indication or contraindication list.* What it does contain:
- General: severe infection, cancer, gross vascular or endocrine disease may contraindicate manual treatment or be better treated otherwise. Avoid forceful technique where pathology is possible. [OD ch. 1–2]
- OAO: treat only where there is "a reasonable expectation of therapeutic benefit". Informed consent is ongoing, and the patient may refuse any part of treatment. [OAO 1.9, 4.x]
- *[not in slice, practitioner to verify]* A standard cranial screen Jarvis should prompt for: acute head injury or suspected skull fracture; suspected intracranial bleed, aneurysm or raised intracranial pressure; recent stroke; uncontrolled seizure disorder; recent cranial or neurosurgery or a shunt; acute vertigo or neuro deficits with no medical work-up. **Any new neurological sign → refer before treating.**

## 5. Patient-education lines in Still's framing (OAO-compliant)

OAO constraints: in Ontario use the title "Osteopathic Manual Practitioner" ("osteopath" is restricted). Make no guarantees of success, no superlatives and no testimonials. Do not claim to "treat conditions". [OAO 1.x, 8.x; OD]

- "Your body is built to look after itself. My job is to find where something is getting in the way and clear it, so your own systems can do the work." (Still: "He seeks the cause, removes the obstruction and lets Nature's remedy… be the doctor.") [R&P]
- "Still's rule was that the blood has to move freely, in and out. Much of what we do is about circulation and drainage, not just about bones." ("The rule of the artery is absolute.") [AUTO]
- "Anyone can find what's wrong. I'm also looking for what's working well, so we can build on it." ("To find health should be the object of the doctor. Anyone can find disease.") [PMP/PHIL]
- "The tension in your back may be your body *protecting* something, possibly an organ that shares its nerve supply. That's why I ask about digestion, breathing and bladder as well as your back." [TAS ch. III; POT]
- "Some of what I find are old adaptations your body has already made peace with. I'll leave those alone." [TAS13 ch. XVI]
- "Once a joint is moving properly I stop. More isn't better." (find it, fix it, leave it alone) [LES]
- "If this keeps coming back, it's usually because the cause sits somewhere else: posture at work, sleep, food, stress. Let's look at those together." (secondary lesions return while their cause continues) [TAS ch. II; LES Kemp]
- "Think of three pillars: how your body is put together, how its systems are running, and what your life is asking of it. We'll work on all three." [LES Kemp]
- "Some symptoms need a medical check first. That's part of doing this safely, not a sign that something is wrong." [OD; OAO 1.6]
